# Walking the model with the owner

A capability map is only as good as its calibration, and calibration comes from the owner, not from
your first pass. The method below came out of a 16-capability interview that changed five targets and
added a capability the model was missing.

---

## Targets before current scores

**Walk the targets first.** The owner's instruction was explicit: *"I think we have to talk more about
the targets for now! So let's focus on those, and not on the current state."*

Two reasons it works in that order. Targets **size the ask** — they are what the asset is arguing for.
And they **set the calibration bar**: once you know what a 4 means to this owner in this model, every
current score has a reference point.

---

## One capability per turn

The owner set the cadence: *"I want you to interview me, so we can make the scores more accurate. Let's
do it question for question and look where we land."*

Per capability, in one turn:

1. State your **proposed target**.
2. Give the **reasoning in one or two lines**.
3. Name the **specific judgement call** you are unsure about.
4. Ask.

**Invite correction of the reasoning, not the number.** "Should this be 3 or 4?" gets you a number.
"I put 4 because the top-level property looks load-bearing to the pitch — is it?" gets you the model.

Do not batch. A list of sixteen proposed targets gets skimmed and approved wholesale, which is exactly
the calibration you were trying to obtain.

---

## Derive the 3-vs-4 bar from the first answer, then hold it

Mid-interview the owner asked: *"could you explain the difference between 4 and 3 to me? I think it is
not clear enough."* The rubric was unusable because it was written in adjectives — "mature", "general",
"self-service".

**Restate every level as concrete observable ability**, never as an adjective:

> At 3, the user can do A, B, and C themselves, and hits a wall outside them.
> At 4, the wall is gone: D and E are also self-service, and nobody files a ticket for them.

Useful default once the bar is set: **a 4 is earned when the top-level property is load-bearing to the
pitch. A 3 is the default.** Hold every subsequent capability against that same bar.

When the owner hesitates between two levels, that is the signal to restate both as observable ability
rather than to argue for one.

---

## Expect to be graded down

In practice the owner pulled **four** proposed 4s down to 3 and pushed one 3 up to 4. Under-calling is
the norm and it is a good sign — the owner knows what "leading" would actually require.

Treat one deliberately low target as the map's **honesty anchor** (see `scoring-discipline.md`).

---

## The model is open, not closed

Mid-interview the owner raised an adjacent concern the model did not cover — tenancy and user roles.
The right move was **not** to stretch a neighbouring capability to absorb it.

When the owner raises a concern:

1. **Test whether it fits an existing capability's *definition*** — the definition as written, not as
   you could argue it.
2. If it is adjacent but outside, **say so plainly** and offer to add a capability. Stretched
   definitions destroy MECE and make the grade meaningless.
3. Separate distinct concerns rather than bundling them — three concerns were raised in one sentence
   there, and two were genuinely new.

---

## Adding or removing a capability is a model change

It ripples further than it looks. Adding one capability touched nine files' worth of surfaces. Work
the checklist:

- the **MODEL** and its layer grouping
- the **NOTES** entry
- the **DETAIL** rubric
- the **components / sub-items** list
- the **greenfield seed** (`GREEN_CUR`)
- **any chart that iterates the set** — the radar, the legend, the averages
- the **sibling artifacts' copies** of the data (a heatmap in the offering asset, for instance)
- **every stated capability *count*** in prose, digit and word form ("sixteen capabilities across four
  layers")
- the **terminology ledger**

Then re-validate. A stale count in a sibling artifact is the classic survivor.
