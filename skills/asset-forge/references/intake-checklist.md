# Intake checklist

Run these questions at the start of any engagement. Ask for what is missing — do not assume answers and proceed. The goal is a shared picture of the asset, the audience, and the constraints before any framing or writing begins.

---

## Asset type

- What is being produced? (RfP/RfQ response, pitch deck, point of view, readout, internal briefing)
- Is this a competitive tender with a formal submission process?
- Is this branded — whose logo, fonts, and design tokens appear in the output, and can the user supply that brand kit?

*If the asset type is unclear, show the user the options and ask them to pick one before proceeding.*

---

## Brief and source documents

- What brief or source documents exist? Ask the user to share them.
- Is there an RfQ or RfP document? If yes: how many requirement clusters does it have? Is there a scoring methodology stated?
- Are there previous presentations, earlier proposal drafts, or a prior-round document?
- Are there competitor or market documents relevant to this engagement?

*Do not start framing without at least a brief. If no brief exists, treat the first session as a brief-building conversation and take notes that become the intake summary.*

---

## Side-channel intelligence

Side-channel intel is often more valuable than the formal brief. Ask:

- Has there been a conversation with the client outside the RfP process? What was learned?
- Do you know who wrote the RfQ and what their real priorities are (as opposed to the stated requirements)?
- Is there a relationship at the client — a coach, sponsor, or internal champion who wants you to win?
- Have you heard anything about competition: who else is bidding, or who the client already considers the frontrunner?
- Is there history with this client — prior engagements, a previous bid, a reason you were or were not shortlisted?

*If the user has no side-channel intel, note that gap explicitly. Do not proceed as if the formal brief is the complete picture.*

---

## Stakeholder and buyer map

Build this explicitly. Do not leave it implicit.

| Role | Question to ask the user |
|---|---|
| Economic buyer | Who signs the contract or approves the budget for this purchase? |
| Technical buyer | Who evaluates the technical solution and will own the platform or output? |
| Coach | Who inside the client organization wants you to win, or will help you navigate internal dynamics? |
| Blocker | Who is likely to push back, and what is their concern? |
| Evaluators (if tender) | Who scores the written response? Are they the same people as the technical buyer? |

For each unknown role: flag it as a risk and ask the user what they can find out before the submission deadline.

---

## Submission mechanics (tender responses only)

- What is the submission deadline?
- Is there a page limit or word count?
- Is there a mandated template or portal form? If yes: get it before writing any prose — the structure must conform to it.
- What format is required for submission: Word, PDF, a portal upload?
- Is there a formal clarification window for questions to the client? If yes: what is the deadline?
- Are there scoring criteria or weightings stated in the RfQ?

*If submission mechanics are unclear, state each gap explicitly in the state file and flag what decisions it blocks. Missing a mandated template mid-production is expensive.*

---

## How to handle missing context

When something above is unknown:

1. State what is missing in plain language.
2. Ask the user directly for the information, or ask what they can realistically find out and by when.
3. Do not assume a reasonable default and proceed silently.
4. If the user genuinely cannot provide the information, record the gap in `asset-forge-state.md` and note which downstream decisions it blocks.

The intake phase ends when you have enough to make a reasoned bid/no-bid decision — not when you have complete information. Perfect information rarely arrives before the qualify gate needs to pass.
