# Gates

Three discipline rules. Each gate is a checkpoint where the cost of proceeding without agreement is high and the cost of stopping briefly is low. The tables below show the most common rationalizations for skipping each gate and why they do not hold. The red-flags list names the specific thoughts that mean you are about to skip.

---

## Gate 1 — Qualify (after Intake, before Frame)

**What it requires.** An explicit go/no-go decision from the user, based on a genuine assessment of win probability and walk-away criteria, before any framing or writing begins.

**Letter.** The user says "go, let's proceed."

**Spirit.** The user has seen the win-probability assessment and the walk-away criteria check, understood the recommendation, and made a conscious decision. A reflexive "yes, let's go" after no assessment is not a pass.

### Rationalization table

| Rationalization | Reality |
|---|---|
| "We already know we're bidding — just start." | Starting without a qualify pass skips the one moment when walk-away criteria are evaluated while the cost of stopping is still low. Two weeks of work is a higher cost than fifteen minutes of assessment. |
| "There's no real competition here." | Win probability still depends on client relationship, brief maturity, and scope fit. Competition is one factor; the others still apply independently. |
| "The deadline is too tight for a qualify step." | A qualify assessment takes fifteen minutes. Discovering a walk-away criterion two weeks in, after significant effort is sunk, costs far more. |
| "We've worked with this client before — we know them." | Familiarity does not replace an explicit assessment. Prior work can strengthen your position or create assumptions that no longer hold; the assessment surfaces which. |
| "The user seems confident — the answer must be go." | The user's confidence is an input to the assessment, not the assessment itself. Surface the factors anyway. |

### Red flags

You are about to skip Gate 1 if you find yourself thinking any of the following:

- "We both know we're doing this — let's just move."
- "I'll do the qualify in my head."
- "The user is excited about this deal, so the answer must be go."
- "We can always pull out later if it looks bad."

## Gate 2 — Frame + Storyline (after Frame, before Build)

**What it requires.** Three things locked before polished asset production begins:
1. **The positioning frame** — a one-line statement of what this asset is *for* and, crucially, what it is *not*: the frame it takes ("positioned as an AI **enabler**, not a guardrail/governance pitch"). This is the decision that, taken wrong, forces a whole-asset reframe after build.
2. **The governing thought** — a single declarative sentence, the Answer to the reader's key Question.
3. **The two-level outline** — the section spine (for exec/DACH audiences, often a WHY/WHAT/HOW spine) with action-title headings.

The positioning frame comes from the `positioning` skill and is recorded in the state file's decision log; the governing thought and outline come from `storyline`. All three are signed off together.

**This gate is iterative.** Producing a strawman — the frame line, a rough governing thought, and a two-level outline — for alignment is encouraged and expected. Rebuilding a strawman several times is normal. Sinking effort into a polished visual build before these three are agreed is what this gate prevents.

**Letter.** The user approves the positioning frame line, the governing thought (a single declarative sentence), and the two-level outline.

**Spirit.** The frame and governing thought have been tested against the SCQA frame. The user has seen the argument structure and confirmed it is the right frame and the right argument for this audience and this moment. Minor title adjustments after this point are fine; a fundamentally different frame or argument is not — that is the failure this gate exists to catch, because it is discovered only after an expensive build otherwise.

**What the iterative loop looks like.** Draft a governing thought. Share it. If the user pushes back, revise the governing thought first, then the argument tree, then the titles. Do not build slides or polished visuals to illustrate an alternative argument — that is a full Build, not a strawman. A strawman is quick and rough; a full build is a large time investment.

### Rationalization table

| Rationalization | Reality |
|---|---|
| "The frame is obvious — of course it's about X." | The frame taken wrong (e.g. building a guardrail/governance pitch when the asset should sell an enabler) invalidates every section. It is the single most expensive thing to discover after build. Lock the one-line frame explicitly. |
| "We're in a hurry — let's just build and fix the story later." | Rebuilding a polished deck because the governing thought was wrong takes more time than the gate saves. The strawman iteration is the fast path. |
| "The storyline is basically obvious from the positioning." | Obvious to whom? The evaluator reads the document, not the positioning notes. The governing thought needs to be explicit, stated, and agreed — not obvious in retrospect. |
| "The user said 'looks good' to the outline — that's enough." | "Looks good" to a rough outline is not the same as approving the governing thought as a specific claim sentence. Get the sentence agreed explicitly. |
| "The client just wants the deck built quickly." | They care about whether the deck works when it lands. The governing-thought gate is what makes it work. |
| "I'll just draft a few slides to show what I mean." | That is not a few slides; that is a Build. A strawman is a governing thought, a two-level outline, and optionally an ASCII mockup — not visual production. |

### Red flags

You are about to skip Gate 2 if you find yourself thinking any of the following:

- "I'll draft a few slides first so the user can see what I mean."
- "The governing thought will become clear once the build is done."
- "The user is impatient — I'll just start building."
- "I can always reframe it in the executive summary later."
- "The storyline is fine, we've talked about it enough."

## Gate 3 — Review (after Build, before Done)

**What it requires.** The asset-review skill must complete — persona critique, redline, humanization, claims check — before the asset is declared done. For tender responses, compliance-matrix sign-off is part of this gate.

**Letter.** The asset-review skill has run and the redline has been applied to the draft.

**Spirit.** The review found and addressed the real issues: capability claims that are aspirational but stated as fact, altitude mismatches that would lose the evaluator at the threshold read, win themes asserted without supporting evidence, and prose that reads as AI-generated. These are addressed in the draft, not acknowledged and deferred to a next version.

### Rationalization table

| Rationalization | Reality |
|---|---|
| "This is an internal document — review is overkill." | Internal documents reach audiences who form judgments about quality and credibility. A weak internal POV shapes how the team is perceived going forward. |
| "We've been writing it for two weeks — we know where the gaps are." | Two weeks of writing produces familiarity blindness. The persona passes surface the issues you stopped seeing because you have read the draft too many times. |
| "The deadline is tomorrow." | Shorten the review, not skip it. One exec-persona pass and a claims check takes under an hour and catches the disqualifying issues. |
| "The client will give feedback in the next round." | The next round may not happen if the first-round submission fails the threshold read. The review exists to prevent that. |
| "The user said it's fine, so it must be." | The user wrote it. They are not the right judge of whether it reads well to someone encountering it cold. |

### Red flags

You are about to skip Gate 3 if you find yourself thinking any of the following:

- "We can tighten it up after we send."
- "It's close enough — let's ship."
- "The review will just slow us down at this point."
- "We've already reviewed it informally."
- "I'll flag the review as a follow-up."
